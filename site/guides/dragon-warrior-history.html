From Dragon Quest to Dragon Warrior: how the game reached America

The game this site calls Dragon Warrior is the first Dragon Quest. It has two names because it was published twice, three years apart, for two audiences.

  1. 1986Dragon Quest comes out for the Famicom in Japan
  2. 1989Nintendo publishes it in North America as Dragon Warrior
  3. 1990Nintendo Power sends the game to its subscribers
  4. 2000The Game Boy Color port reaches North America
  5. 2014A phone version is released worldwide
  6. 2019The game comes to the Nintendo Switch
  7. 2025The HD-2D remake is released

Japan, 1986

Dragon Quest came out for the Famicom on May 27, 1986. Enix published it and Chunsoft built it. Yuji Horii designed and wrote it, Akira Toriyama drew the monsters and characters, and Koichi Sugiyama wrote the music. Koichi Nakamura directed and programmed it.

Horii took two ideas from American computer games. The fights seen through the hero's eyes came from Wizardry, and the view of the world from above came from Ultima. What he added was simplicity: one hero, one enemy at a time, and a short menu of commands.

The original sold 1.5 million copies in Japan, more than a million of them in the first six months.

North America, 1989

Nintendo published the game in North America in August 1989. It could not be called Dragon Quest there. A pen-and-paper role-playing game named DragonQuest already held that name in the United States, so the game became Dragon Warrior. The series kept that name in America for many years.

The three years between the two releases show in the game. The North American version is not a plain translation.

The biggest change is saving. The Japanese game gave you a password to write down. The American cartridge has a battery and keeps three save files.

It also looks different. In Japan the hero and the townspeople always faced the player. In the American release they turn to face the way they walk, and shorelines were drawn where land meets water. Toriyama's drawings in the manual and on the box were replaced with a more Western style.

The text was rewritten in a mock old English, full of "thou" and "thy". Places and people were renamed, and each spell got a one-word English name such as HEAL or HURT.

Some jokes were removed as well. A woman who offers a "puff-puff" in the Japanese towns sells tomatoes in the American one.

The giveaway

In late 1990, Nintendo reportedly sent free copies to people who subscribed to its magazine, Nintendo Power, together with a 64-page Explorer's Handbook. A subscription cost 20 dollars. The game alone cost about 50 in shops.

The offer brought the magazine nearly 500,000 new subscribers. It also put the cartridge into a great many homes. About 500,000 copies were sold in the United States.

After the NES

The game has been remade many times. A Game Boy Color port came to North America in 2000. Versions for phones followed in 2014 and for the Nintendo Switch in 2019. An HD-2D remake was released on October 30, 2025.

The remakes changed more than the graphics. Names were translated again, and on the Game Boy Color the hero's ancestor is called Loto, as in Japan, and not Erdrick. This site and its walkthrough use the names of the 1989 NES release throughout: Erdrick, Gwaelin, Tantegel.

Why the NES version still gets played

It is short. A full run takes somewhere between ten and twenty-five hours, depending on who you ask. The whole world fits on one map, there is one save point, and every rule can be learned in the first hour.
